Turning one-by-one operations into safe bulk actions

Operations agents processed payment requests and account locks one account at a time. I designed the servicing platform they work in and the bulk actions that replaced that work.

Role
Product design, from workflow mapping to shipped UI
Team
2 designers, ~8 engineers, Director of Product
Timeline
2023–2025
Users
40–50 operations and underwriting staff

Visuals are reconstructions built with Kestrel UI, a fictional brand with fictional data. They show the principles of the work, not the company’s actual screens.

Context

Servicing a single small-business loan meant jumping between separate tools: account summaries, requests, documents and cash-flow analysis each lived in their own place. Quantum Hub brought them into one platform, and I designed its UI foundations.

The problem

Agents handled around 70 accounts a day (my estimate). I interviewed and observed them directly, and ran sessions with operations stakeholders to map how they actually worked. Two problems came up:

One account at a time

Payment requests and account locks were processed one by one: open, review, act, confirm, back to the list.

Found in: observation and interviews
No clear feedback

The tools didn’t say clearly what would happen, what had failed or what an agent was allowed to do.

Who we designed for

Operations agent

Goal: process the day’s volume quickly and without errors, sure that each change hit the right account.

Frustration: repeating the same cycle account by account, with a system that didn’t say what would happen, what failed or what they were allowed to do.

Operations manager

Goal: the team clears the day’s volume with control: what changed, what failed, and each agent doing only what they’re allowed to.

Frustration: with no report per batch, errors showed up late and had to be checked account by account.

What went first

We prioritized by how often each task happened. Ongoing feedback also came through operations managers, and our design team turned it into its own tickets.

First

Payment requests and account locks: the main volume.

Also competing

Draw requests, and approvals and rejections of applications in progress.

Key challenges

One action per batch

  2. DefineStart with the most frequent tasks: payment requests and account locks.
  3. DevelopSelect, then act: choose many accounts in the table and run a single action.
  4. DeliverBulk actions replaced one-by-one processing for both tasks, and shipped.

No clear feedback

Checked before, reported after
  1. DiscoverObservation and interviews: unclear outcomes, failures found late, permissions not explained.
  2. DefineAgents verify before and after each action; managers see what changed and what failed.
  3. DevelopConfirmation before, a result after, failures listed by account, disabled actions that explain why.

Each challenge runs through the double diamond: Discover and Define in cobalt, Develop and Deliver in magenta.

Before: one record at a time

  1. Back to the list, every time. Acting on an account meant opening it, then returning to the list for the next one.
  2. An action that didn’t say what it would do. No summary of what was about to change before running it.
  3. Restrictions without a reason. An agent couldn’t tell why an action wasn’t available.
  4. One message for the outcome. When something failed, the agent didn’t learn what or why.

Decisions

Select, then act

Agents choose many accounts in the table and run a single action, instead of opening each record.

Ask before, report after

Every bulk action asked for confirmation before running and reported the result after, so agents could verify what they were about to change and what actually changed.

Failures stay visible

When some accounts failed, the result listed which ones, instead of reporting a single success or a single error for the whole batch.

Permissions, surfaced

Actions an agent couldn’t perform were disabled, with a tooltip explaining the restriction.

Outcome

  • Bulk actions replaced one-by-one processing for payment requests and account locks, and shipped.
  • The Director of Product credited them with significant efficiency gains in servicing workflows.

How I’d validate it

Proposal, not run in the project

Who

5 operations agents, following Nielsen: with 5 users most usability problems show up.

How

Unmoderated tests with the prototype, on the desktop setup agents use.

Tasks

Process a batch of payment requests, lock several accounts, find which accounts failed and why.

Metrics

Success rate, time per task, errors on the wrong account, and SEQ (ease from 1 to 7 after each task).

What I’d do differently

Measure time per task before and after launch. The gain was clear to the people using it, but I can’t put a number on it.

The shipped version also left room to improve, and I explored the next iteration in Kestrel UI:

  • Say why, and let agents retry. Failed accounts showed a general error. Each failure should state its reason and offer to retry only the failed ones.
  • Friction where it matters. Locking an account used the same confirmation as any other action. High-risk actions deserve a Risk modal that the backdrop can’t dismiss, with focus starting on Cancel.
  • Explain restrictions in place. Tooltips carried a generic permission message. The reason, and who can unblock the action, should be visible without hovering.
